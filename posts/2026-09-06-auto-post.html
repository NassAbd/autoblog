Introduction

The recent lawsuits filed by The Seattle Times and Newsday against OpenAI and Microsoft have reignited a fierce debate over the use of copyrighted news content in generative AI models. While the headlines focus on the legal battle, the ripple effects extend far beyond the courtroom. This post examines the broader context, the evolving legal landscape, and the strategic choices facing both publishers and AI developers. By looking past the immediate drama, we can better understand how this conflict could reshape the future of journalism, technology, and the very notion of intellectual property in the age of large language models (LLMs).

1. The Immediate Trigger: How the Lawsuits Were Filed

  • Plaintiffs: The Seattle Times and Newsday—two mid‑size U.S. newspapers with strong regional influence.
  • Defendants: OpenAI (creator of ChatGPT and the GPT‑4‑Turbo series) and Microsoft (the primary cloud partner and commercial distributor of OpenAI’s services).
  • Core Allegation: The defendants allegedly trained their models on the plaintiffs’ copyrighted articles without permission, then used the resulting knowledge to generate news‑style outputs that compete with the original publications.
  • Relief Sought: Injunctive relief to halt the use of the newspapers’ content in training data, statutory damages for copyright infringement, and a requirement that any AI‑generated excerpts be properly attributed and compensated.

The lawsuits echo earlier actions taken by The New York Times, The Washington Post, and Associated Press—all of which have pursued similar claims against AI firms. However, the Seattle Times and Newsday bring fresh perspectives: they represent smaller, regional outlets that rely heavily on subscription revenue and have less leverage in negotiations with tech giants.

2. Legal Landscape: From Fair Use to “Data Scraping”

2.1 Copyright and Machine Learning

U.S. copyright law traditionally protects the expression of ideas, not the ideas themselves. Courts have been split on whether feeding large datasets into an LLM constitutes a “fair use” of the underlying works. Key factors include:

  1. Purpose and Character of Use – Transformative uses (e.g., creating a summary) may favor fair use, while direct replication does not.
  2. Nature of the Copyrighted Work – Factual news articles receive less protection than creative works.
  3. Amount and Substantiality – Training on millions of articles may be seen as a substantial portion, but each individual excerpt is tiny.
  4. Effect on the Market – If AI outputs replace human‑written articles, the market harm argument strengthens.

The Google Books case (Authors Guild v. Google) set a precedent that large‑scale digitization for search purposes could be fair use, but the commercial nature of generative AI adds a new twist.

2.2 The “Scraping” Debate

Many AI firms rely on web crawlers to collect publicly available data. Plaintiffs argue that “scraping” copyrighted content without explicit permission violates the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) and the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA). Defendants counter that publicly accessible webpages are fair game under the Terms of Service doctrine, a stance that has been contested in multiple recent rulings.

2.3 International Perspectives

  • European Union: The upcoming AI Act proposes a “training data” exemption for publicly available data, but also mandates transparency and possible compensation for copyrighted works.
  • Australia & Canada: Both jurisdictions are watching the U.S. outcomes closely, as they have similar “fair dealing” provisions that could be interpreted in either direction.

3. Why This Matters for Journalism

3.1 Revenue Erosion

Regional newspapers already face shrinking ad revenues and subscription churn. If AI models can generate “good enough” news snippets, readers may turn to free AI chat interfaces instead of paying for a subscription. This could accelerate the decline of local news ecosystems.

3.2 Credibility and Trust

AI‑generated content can inadvertently spread misinformation if the model misinterprets or fabricates details. When a trusted outlet’s style is replicated without attribution, the line between original reporting and AI‑driven synthesis blurs, potentially harming the outlet’s reputation.

3.3 Content Ownership and Control

Publishers are confronting a fundamental question: Who owns the data that fuels AI? By treating their articles as a commodity, they can negotiate licensing deals; by treating them as public domain, they risk losing control over how their brand is represented.

4. The Tech Industry’s Response

4.1 Licensing Initiatives

Microsoft has announced a “News Content Partnership Program” that offers publishers a revenue‑share model for the use of their articles in training data. Early adopters receive a 2‑5% share of API usage fees tied to their content. Critics argue the rates are low and the opt‑in process opaque.

4.2 Technical Mitigations

OpenAI has begun experimenting with “data watermarking”—embedding subtle signals in training data that can later be detected in model outputs. While still in prototype, this could provide a forensic tool to prove whether a particular article was used in training.

4.3 Transparency Dashboards

Both OpenAI and Microsoft are rolling out public dashboards that list the top domains used for model training. The dashboards aim to demonstrate good faith, but they also expose the sheer scale of data ingestion—often encompassing millions of news URLs.
